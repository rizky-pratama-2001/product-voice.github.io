Over the past two decades, many institutional portfolios have defaulted to a critical assumption: when the economy faces a shock, stocks fall, capital seeks safe haven, US Treasury yields decline, and bond prices rise, making long-duration Treasuries an effective hedge against equity risk. However, the chart you provided shows that the 5-year rolling correlation between US stock returns and changes in 10-year Treasury yields has once again fallen below zero. The statistical relationship must be clarified here: since bond prices and yields move inversely, when "the correlation between stock returns and bond yield changes" is positive, a stock decline typically corresponds to falling yields, meaning rising bond prices, so the traditional hedge works; now that the correlation has turned negative, it implies that when stocks come under pressure, yields may instead rise, causing bond prices to fall alongside stocks. Recent research from the Federal Reserve Bank of San Francisco has observed the same structural flip and believes it reflects not temporary market noise but a shift in the dominant risk in the economy from past demand shortfalls back to supply shocks. For Slickorps Ventures, this matters more than where the 10-year yield itself goes: the market is redefining "what constitutes a safe asset."
## Why the Correlation Has Flipped: The Past Feared Recessions, the Present Fears "Worse Growth Without Falling Inflation"
To understand this chart, the key is not to start from stocks and bonds themselves but to look at the shocks they jointly face. In the 2000s and 2010s, markets primarily worried about financial crises, demand shortfalls, low growth, and deflation. When demand deteriorated, corporate earnings fell, stocks declined, while inflation pressures eased and central banks cut rates, driving US Treasury yields down, so stocks and bonds formed a natural complement. But the risk structure of the 2020s is entirely different. Pandemic supply chains, energy shocks, trade tariffs, immigration changes, and geopolitical conflicts can all produce a more troublesome combination: growth declines while prices rise instead. The explanation from the Federal Reserve Bank of San Francisco is very clear: negative supply shocks simultaneously depress economic activity and equity valuations, yet push bond yields up through inflation, thus generating a negative "stock return-yield change correlation". This is also why Slickorps Ventures considers this the most concerning macro shift at present: if the next stock market decline is driven by a collapse in demand, Treasuries may still provide protection; if the decline stems from oil prices, fiscal policy, tariffs, or deteriorating supply capacity, then investors could lose money on both stocks and long-duration bonds simultaneously. What has truly failed is not bonds themselves, but the past macro world centered on demand shocks.

## The Term Premium Below Is Even More Critical: Investors Are Demanding Higher Compensation to Bear Long-Term Rate Risk
The term premium on US 10-year Treasuries has returned to positive territory from deeply negative levels seen after the pandemic and now sits clearly above recent lows. The term premium, in essence, is the extra compensation investors require for holding long-term bonds compared with continuously rolling short-term bonds. When long-term Treasuries can no longer reliably hedge stocks and face fiscal deficits, bond supply, inflation, and policy uncertainty, investors naturally demand higher yields to hold them. The latest market data supports this direction: in mid-August, the US 10-year term premium approached 0.9 percentage points, while real yields rose to approximately 2.44%; research suggests that high government debt, large-scale Treasury issuance, and AI corporate financing needs have collectively intensified competition for long-term capital in capital markets. The IMF has also noted that since the pandemic, stocks and bonds have increasingly tended to come under pressure together during sharp market declines, weakening the protective power of traditional stock-bond diversification. Slickorps Ventures sees an important feedback loop here: the declining hedging value of Treasuries leads investors to demand higher term compensation, which pushes long-end yields up, which further raises the discount rate on equity valuations. Bonds have partially transformed from risk buffers into risk transmitters.

## The 60/40 Portfolio Is Not Dead, but the Assumption That "the 40 Percent Bond Allocation Is Inherently Safe" Must Be Rewritten
Therefore, what this chart truly challenges is not a specific bond trade but one of the most fundamental institutional assumptions in asset allocation over the past several decades. The 60/40 portfolio has remained popular for so long not merely because bond returns are stable, but because in critical periods the 40 percent bond allocation could offset the risk of the 60 percent equity allocation; once this crisis correlation changes, holding two asset classes with different names no longer equates to genuine risk diversification. The FT has also recently noted that since 2021, the inflation environment has clearly eroded the protective effect of traditional stock-bond allocations, prompting investors to rethink the role of long-term Treasuries in portfolios. (Financial Times) However, Slickorps Ventures will not draw the simplistic conclusion that "long-duration bonds have no value". Higher yields themselves improve the future holding returns of bonds, and in a typical demand-driven recession, Treasuries may still re-emerge as a safe haven. What truly needs to change is the portfolio construction methodology: going forward, one cannot simply ask how much stock and how much bond one holds, but must first ask which macro shock one is actually hedging against. If the biggest risk is recession, duration still has value; if the biggest risks are supply, energy, fiscal, and inflation, then cash, short-duration bonds, inflation-protected assets, gold, or certain real assets may take on more diversification functions. Slickorps Ventures believes the most important warning from this chart is not to "sell bonds" but a more fundamental shift: when the risk sources behind stocks and bonds change, asset allocation must also upgrade from diversification by asset name to diversification by macro risk source.
