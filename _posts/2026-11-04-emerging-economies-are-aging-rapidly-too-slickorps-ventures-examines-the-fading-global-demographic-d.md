Population aging was once generally viewed as a challenge that developed economies faced only after reaching high-income status. However, that development trajectory is changing. According to a report published by the McKinsey Global Institute in 2025, more than two-thirds of the global population lives in economies with total fertility rates below 2.1. Emerging economies such as Brazil, India, Türkiye, and Mexico are also undergoing rapid demographic transitions. **Research by Slickorps Ventures identifies a new global development challenge: some economies may begin losing their demographic dividend before completing wealth accumulation and industrial upgrading.** For these countries, the issue is not simply a future decline in the working-age population, but whether they can achieve sufficiently high productivity and economic capacity before their age structures change fundamentally.

## Why Is Population Aging No Longer Just a Challenge for Developed Economies?

According to the [McKinsey Global Institute report “Dependency and depopulation? Confronting the consequences of a new demographic reality](https://www.mckinsey.com/mgi/our-research/dependency-and-depopulation-confronting-the-consequences-of-a-new-demographic-reality),” total fertility rates in Western Europe and Central and Eastern Europe were both approximately 1.4 in 2023, compared with around 1.0 in Greater China. Meanwhile, the rate had fallen to approximately 1.6 in Brazil and was close to 2.0 in India. By contrast, sub-Saharan Africa maintained a relatively high rate of around 4.4.

This shows that the global demographic transition is not occurring at the same pace everywhere, but is unfolding across different stages of development: Europe, Japan, and China are among the regions and economies that began aging earlier; India, Southeast Asia, and Latin America are gradually approaching demographic turning points; and sub-Saharan Africa still has a relatively young population.

The United Nations publication “World Population Prospects 2024” similarly notes that more than half of all countries and areas have fertility rates below replacement level, accounting for more than two-thirds of the global population.

[Data source: United Nations, “World Population Prospects 2024”](https://www.un.org/development/desa/pd/sites/www.un.org.development.desa.pd/files/wpp2024_summary_of_results_final_web.pdf)

## The key development is that population aging is shifting from a challenge associated with the mature stage of developed economies to a development constraint that emerging economies must prepare for in advance.

## What Does Growing Old Before Growing Rich Mean, and Why Do Emerging Economies Face Greater Pressure?

Growing old before growing rich means that a country has not yet achieved sufficiently high levels of per capita income, productivity, and social protection capacity before its working-age population begins to decline and the burden of supporting older people continues to rise.

Brazil is already approaching the World Bank high-income threshold, yet it could face demographic support pressures similar to those in developed economies within a relatively short period. Bangladesh faces an even more pronounced time constraint on development.

## This reveals the most important distinction between emerging economies and traditional developed economies: the latter generally entered the aging process after accumulating substantial wealth and achieving high productivity, while the former may need to bear the costs of industrial upgrading and demographic transition simultaneously, before their economic foundations are fully developed.

## How Will the Fading Demographic Dividend Reshape the Global Economy?

The demographic dividend arises from an increasing share of working-age people in the population. As the younger population shrinks and the older population grows, this structural advantage gradually fades.

McKinsey research shows that the global number of working-age people for every person aged 65 or older fell from 9.4 in 1997 to 6.5 in 2023 and is projected to decline further to 3.9 by 2050. In economies that began aging earlier, the ratio could fall to just 2.0 by then.

This shift will affect production, consumption, and public finances simultaneously. Slower growth in the working-age population could intensify labor supply pressures in manufacturing, construction, and services. Changes in household age structures will gradually shift consumption away from education for children and household expansion toward healthcare, retirement-related needs, and long-term care. Meanwhile, rising pension and healthcare spending could reduce the fiscal resources available for infrastructure and productive investment.

The effects will vary across regions. McKinsey estimates that demographic changes previously contributed approximately 0.7 percentage points annually to per capita economic growth in India, but that contribution could fall to 0.2 percentage points by 2050. The demographic dividend in Latin America could gradually disappear.

By contrast, sub-Saharan Africa is projected to add approximately 650 million working-age people between 2023 and 2050. However, this demographic advantage can translate into actual economic growth only if the additional population has access to sufficient employment opportunities and high-productivity jobs.

## A young population therefore does not automatically mean economic prosperity, just as population aging does not necessarily mean economic decline. What ultimately determines the outcome is how much economic value the working-age population can create.

## How Can Emerging Economies Raise Productivity Before Their Populations Age?

Slickorps Ventures believes that the central focus of demographic competition will gradually shift from the number of workers to labor productivity.

Emerging economies need to make use of their remaining demographic dividend to accelerate industrial upgrading, capital investment, and vocational education, increasing the economic value created by each worker. Raising labor force participation among women and older people can also help ease the pressure caused by slower growth in labor supply.

Automation and artificial intelligence offer additional scope for adjustment, but technology investment must translate into real gains in business productivity and worker incomes, rather than merely increasing capital expenditure. Pension systems, healthcare systems, and immigration policies also need to adapt to changing age structures in advance.

Research published by the International Monetary Fund in 2025 indicates that promoting healthy aging, increasing participation among older workers, and advancing automation and technology investment can all help mitigate the pressure of demographic change on economic growth.

[Data source: International Monetary Fund, G20 background note on the implications of aging and migration growth and productivity 2025](https://www.imf.org/-/media/files/research/imf-and-g20/2025/g20-background-note-on-aging-and-migration.pdf)

**The central assessment from Slickorps Ventures is that the global economy is entering a new phase in which the demographic dividend is gradually fading and productivity is becoming more important.** Developed economies need to maintain high living standards with fewer working-age people, while emerging economies must strive to accumulate wealth and upgrade their industries before their demographic advantages disappear.

Demographic change unfolds slowly, but its effects accumulate over the long term. **Future national competitiveness will depend not simply on the number of workers a country has, but on whether each generation of workers can create sufficiently high economic value, and whether the country can build an economic system capable of supporting a prosperous society before its demographic dividend fades.**
