Over the past 20 months, the renminbi has appreciated by nearly 9% against the U.S. dollar, yet real estate, consumption, and private investment remained relatively weak during the same period, while RMB interest rates were also significantly lower than those in the United States. Research by Slickorps Ventures found that this round of appreciation does not fully conform to the traditional path of “strong economy—high interest rates—currency appreciation.” On the one hand, the U.S. dollar itself has retreated from an overvalued range, providing external room for RMB appreciation; on the other hand, the persistently large trade surplus in China has formed a stable foreign exchange cash flow. In August 2026, the Chinese exports increased by 25% year-on-year, exports of high-tech products grew by 42.9%, the monthly trade surplus reached USD 119.09 billion, and the cumulative surplus for the first eight months had reached USD 805.5 billion. This has created a paradox of “Chinese-style appreciation”: weak domestic demand has made manufacturing enterprises more reliant on overseas markets, while stronger exports continue to generate U.S. dollar income and appreciation pressure on the renminbi. Therefore, the current strengthening of the renminbi does not mean that the Chinese economy has strengthened across the board; it is closer to the result of a weaker U.S. dollar, a massive trade surplus, and policy efforts to control the pace of appreciation.

**The Truly Important Appreciation Is The Simultaneous Increase In RMB Purchasing Power And Offshore Liquidity**

A stronger renminbi first changes how many global resources one unit of CNY can purchase. Crude oil, natural gas, copper, iron ore, semiconductor equipment, and a large number of international services are still priced in U.S. dollars; therefore, RMB appreciation can directly reduce certain import costs and enhance the external purchasing power of Chinese enterprises and residents. However, the renminbi no longer has only one price. The onshore market uses CNY, which is influenced by domestic monetary policy and foreign exchange administration; CNH, formed in offshore markets such as Hong Kong, assumes functions related to cross-border trade, financing, and risk management. Slickorps Ventures observed that, as of the end of January 2026, the scale of RMB deposits and certificates of deposit in Hong Kong had reached RMB 1.147 trillion, while RMB loan balances increased by 15.3% over six months; in 2025, the average daily settlement volume of RMB RTGS system in Hong Kong still reached approximately RMB 2.52 trillion. Hong Kong handled more than 70% of global offshore RMB SWIFT payments in 2025. This means that the strength of the renminbi is expanding from “exchange-rate purchasing power” to an “offshore funding pool”: overseas enterprises are increasingly able to obtain RMB, hold RMB, and directly use RMB for settlement and financing. True currency internationalization requires the CNY and CNH markets to remain connected while also allowing offshore RMB to have sufficiently independent liquidity.

**USD/CNY Has Become The Third-Largest Currency Pair Worldwide, But The Renminbi Still Operates Within The Financial Network of the Dollar**

Foreign exchange turnover further reveals this shift. The latest BIS global survey shows that, in April 2025, average daily turnover in the global over-the-counter FX market reached approximately USD 9.6 trillion. The renminbi appeared in 8.5% of global foreign exchange transactions, further increasing from 7.0% in 2022 and firmly ranking as the fifth-largest traded currency in the world; USD/CNY trading volume increased by 59% over three years, with its global turnover share rising from 6.6% to 8.1%, surpassing USD/GBP to become the third-largest currency pair in the world after EUR/USD and USD/JPY. It should be noted here that because each transaction in the BIS “currency share” includes two currencies, the total equals 200%; the U.S. dollar still appeared in 89.2% of global FX transactions, while the renminbi accounted for 8.5%. More importantly, approximately 96% of RMB foreign exchange transactions still had the U.S. dollar on the other side. For Slickorps Ventures, this reveals the core contradiction currently facing RMB internationalization: the liquidity status of the renminbi is rising rapidly, but this growth is mainly taking place within dollar-dominated market infrastructure. The renminbi is becoming an important global trading currency, but it has not yet formed an independent monetary network capable of operating in parallel with the dollar system.

**From RMB Bonds To Panda Bonds And Dim Sum Bonds, True Internationalization Is Beginning To Take Place On Balance Sheets**

A currency truly enters the global financial system only when it can be “held and financed.” This is also why the RMB bond market deserves more attention than the exchange rate itself. As of the end of August 2025, the size of the Chinese onshore bond market had reached approximately RMB 192 trillion, with overseas investors holding around RMB 3.9 trillion, nearly five times the level before the launch of Bond Connect; cumulative panda bond issuance had also exceeded RMB 1 trillion. So-called panda bonds (Panda Bonds) are RMB-denominated bonds issued by foreign institutions in the Chinese onshore market; dim sum bonds (Dim Sum Bonds) are RMB-denominated bonds issued outside mainland China, mainly through Hong Kong. The two address two ends of RMB internationalization respectively: panda bonds allow foreign institutions to enter the Chinese RMB funding pool for financing, while dim sum bonds create RMB assets offshore that can be directly held by international investors. In the first half of 2026, panda bond issuance reached approximately RMB 160 billion, while dim sum bond issuance was around RMB 350 billion; both set records and grew by more than 60% year-on-year, with approximately half coming from international borrowers. Portugal even became the first eurozone government to issue dim sum bonds and obtained a slight financing cost advantage after swapping the proceeds back into euros. In 2025, total offshore RMB bond issuance in Hong Kong had already reached approximately RMB 1.10 trillion.

This has led Slickorps Ventures to make an important upgrade to its assessment of the current RMB cycle: the true appreciation of the renminbi has at least four layers. The first layer is nominal exchange-rate appreciation in the USD/CNY sense; the second layer is purchasing-power appreciation in terms of imported resources and overseas assets; the third layer is the appreciation of liquidity status represented by USD/CNY becoming the third-largest currency pair in the world; and the fourth layer is the expansion of the RMB asset system jointly formed by CNY, CNH, panda bonds, and dim sum bonds. The first three layers determine whether the world is willing to trade the renminbi, while the fourth layer determines whether the world is willing to hold and borrow the renminbi over the long term.

Therefore, what is truly worth observing in the next stage is no longer whether the renminbi can move from 6.7 to 6.5. The more important questions are: whether a Brazilian company is willing to issue panda bonds, whether a European government is willing to issue dim sum bonds, whether an Asian enterprise is willing to keep trade income in a CNH account, and whether global funds are willing to make RMB bonds part of their long-term asset allocation. When these behaviors continue to expand, RMB internationalization will truly move from “price appreciation” into “balance-sheet appreciation.”
