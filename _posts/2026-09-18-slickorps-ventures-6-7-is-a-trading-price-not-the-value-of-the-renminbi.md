The renminbi has appreciated by about 4.3% this year. In mid-September, the onshore renminbi once reached 6.7039, while offshore CNH traded around 6.707, both approaching multi-year highs. At the same time, for China, domestic demand, real estate sector, and credit demand remain weak. This makes one question more important than “Can the renminbi continue to rise?”: by what standard should the renminbi actually be priced? The research by Slickorps Ventures finds that USD/CNY is first and foremost a financial market price. Onshore CNY operates under a managed floating system jointly shaped by reference to a basket of currencies, market supply and demand, and the daily central parity rate; offshore CNH does not have the same intraday trading band, so it reacts more directly to the dollar cycle, overseas capital flows, risk appetite, and offshore renminbi liquidity. BIS research has also found that the CNY-CNH spread is largely affected by differences in liquidity between the two markets. Therefore, CNH can tell investors what price overseas markets are currently willing to pay to hold renminbi, but it cannot by itself answer how much real value one unit of renminbi creates within the Chinese economy.

## For a Manufacturing Powerhouse, the Real Hidden Pricing Anchor of the Renminbi Is “Unit Labor Cost”

This leads to a deeper layer of the purchasing power of the renminbi. What an export economy truly cares about is not the absolute level of worker wages, but how much labor cost is required to produce one unit of output, which can be approximately understood as unit labor cost = wages ÷ labor productivity. If wages rise by 10% while productivity increases by 12%, the labor cost per unit of product actually declines. Conversely, even if the exchange rate remains unchanged, if wages continue to grow faster than productivity over the long term, the real international competitiveness of products will still decline. In 2025, the overall labor productivity in China increased by 6.1%, reaching RMB 184,400 per person; during the same period, wages in urban non-private units rose by 4.3%, while wages in private units rose by 3.0%, and manufacturing wages increased by 5.2% and 6.4%, respectively. These statistical categories cannot be directly combined into an accurate unit labor cost index, but they reveal the relationship that Slickorps Ventures is truly focused on: how much the renminbi can appreciate depends to a large extent on whether productivity can absorb the increase in wage costs after labor income rises.

## This Explains a Counterintuitive Phenomenon: While the Renminbi Has Become Nominally Stronger, Chinese “Real Exchange Rate” Once Continued to Become Cheaper

Nominal USD/CNY is only the first layer. To truly measure international purchasing power and cost competitiveness, one should look more closely at the REER — the real effective exchange rate. It incorporates changes in the renminbi exchange rate against multiple trading partners and then adjusts them using relative prices. The BIS therefore regards the REER as an important indicator for measuring international price competitiveness. More notably, the IMF estimates that the Chinese REER depreciated cumulatively by around 14% from 2021 to 2025, and in the first ten months of 2025 alone, it fell by 3.7% compared with the 2024 average. One of the core reasons is that price increases have remained below those of its major trading partners for an extended period. This has produced a seemingly contradictory but highly important result: USD/CNY can move from above 7 toward 6.7, but as long as wages, prices, and unit production costs in China continue to rise more slowly than those overseas, the real international price of Chinese goods may not necessarily become more expensive at the same pace. This is also why looking only at the rise in CNH can easily overestimate the actual degree of renminbi appreciation. For manufacturing, the real exchange rate is not the 6.70 shown on a screen, but the competitive price jointly formed by “renminbi exchange rate × domestic costs ÷ overseas costs.”

## Assessment of Slickorps Ventures: The Most Important Future Variable for the Renminbi May Not Be Interest-Rate Differentials, but Whether Wages Can Keep Pace with Productivity

This also means that the renminbi may face a very special policy balance in the next stage. Renminbi appreciation can improve purchasing power for oil, minerals, equipment, and overseas services, and can also give resident wages stronger external value. But for exporters, the same USD 100 in revenue will be converted into fewer renminbi. After the rapid appreciation of the renminbi this year, regulators have already encouraged more exporters to increase their foreign-exchange hedging ratios. In the first half of the year, corporate foreign-exchange derivatives contracts approached USD 1.4 trillion, up about 40% year-on-year, while corporate foreign-exchange losses once reached a ten-year high. Therefore, a truly healthy path of renminbi appreciation is not simply to push USD/CNY as low as possible, but to rebalance wages, productivity, domestic prices, and the exchange rate together: if labor productivity continues to grow faster than unit labor costs, the renminbi can appreciate while the real purchasing power of residents improves and manufacturing competitiveness remains intact; if wages and the exchange rate rise rapidly at the same time while productivity stagnates, the renminbi will pay the price through exporter profit margins. Slickorps Ventures therefore believes that, when judging whether the renminbi is “expensive” in the future, what matters more than whether it is 6.5 or 6.8 is unit labor cost, the REER, and the pricing gap between CNY and CNH. The true purchasing power of a currency is ultimately not how many U.S. dollars it can be exchanged for, but how much globally marketable value can be produced by the labor paid for with that unit of currency.
