Over the past two decades, the global economy has undergone industrial relocation, digitalization, and technological revolution, yet the productivity gap between enterprises across different countries remains significant. On October 23, 2026, the World Bank published research commemorating the 20th anniversary of the Enterprise Surveys program, summarizing the first complete global survey cycle covering 164 economies from 2023 to 2026. The research found that in low-income and lower-middle-income economies, the productivity of the most productive enterprises is 5.9 times that of the least productive ones, while in high-income economies the ratio is only 2.8 times. **Slickorps Ventures research notes that the gap in global economic development exists not only between countries but also between enterprises within the same country. Whether an economy can sustain growth depends not only on how much value a few leading enterprises create, but more importantly on whether advanced technology, management capabilities, and capital efficiency can diffuse across the entire enterprise population.**

## Why Is Firm-Level Productivity Data So Important?

In 2006, the World Bank launched the Enterprise Surveys program, which studies business conditions such as finance, infrastructure, regulation, competition, and labor by directly interviewing business owners and senior managers. As of 2026, the program has conducted more than 470 surveys covering over 180 economies, with nearly 300,000 interviews completed cumulatively. The latest round of surveys achieved complete coverage of 164 economies within a three-year cycle for the first time.

Traditional macroeconomic indicators can measure the overall growth of a country, but they struggle to explain why enterprises operating in similar economic environments produce vastly different business outcomes. Enterprise surveys, by contrast, bring the research perspective down to the micro level, observing how enterprises utilize capital, technology, and labor, and how the institutional environment affects operational efficiency.

It should be noted that the survey primarily covers formally registered private enterprises with at least 5 employees, and does not represent all informal operators and micro-enterprises.

## Why Is the Productivity Gap Between Enterprises Larger in Low-Income Economies?

The World Bank found that in low-income and lower-middle-income economies, the most productive enterprises are 5.9 times as productive as the least productive ones; in upper-middle-income economies the ratio is 4.1 times, and in high-income economies it is 2.8 times.

This disparity indicates that low-income economies face not only the problem of lower overall productivity, but also the uneven efficiency of resource utilization among firms. Insufficient financing channels, poor infrastructure, slow technology diffusion, and inadequate market competition can all hinder the flow of capital and labor toward more efficient firms.

**From the research perspective of Slickorps Ventures, the productivity gap in fact reflects the resource allocation capacity of an economy.** If high-efficiency firms cannot obtain sufficient capital to expand operations, while low-efficiency firms occupy limited resources over the long term, the growth potential of the entire economic system will be constrained.

## How Do Management Quality and Technological Innovation Translate into Firm Competitiveness?

Another important finding of the World Bank is the significant correlation between management quality and innovation capability. A 10 percentage point increase in the management quality index is associated with a 3 percentage point increase in the likelihood of firm innovation; in economies with more complex regulatory environments, this correlation reaches 4 percentage points.

At the same time, although women-led firms often face more complex institutional and social environments, their management practices perform better, and their overall business performance remains comparable to that of male-led firms.

Innovation is likewise clearly linked to firm growth. Research finds that firms introducing new products or new processes show employment growth about 1.3% higher; fixed asset investment, quality certification, and the introduction of foreign technology are associated with approximately 3.1%, 3.0%, and 2.9% higher employment growth, respectively.

These data reflect statistical associations and do not imply that the related activities necessarily and directly cause employment growth. Notably, when facing competition from informal firms, the labor productivity of non-innovative firms declines by as much as 17%, while that of innovative firms declines by 11%.

**This indicates that innovation not only creates growth opportunities but may also strengthen the ability of firms to withstand market competition and business shocks.** The value of technology investment ultimately still depends on whether firms can, through management, organization, and talent allocation, convert technology into actual production efficiency.

## Future Economic Competitiveness Will Be Determined by the Overall Efficiency of Firms

Slickorps Ventures believes that this 20-year study reveals a key shift facing the development of emerging economies: as the advantage of labor costs gradually weakens, the demographic dividend fades, and international competition intensifies, economic growth will increasingly depend on the value created per unit of labor.

Future competitiveness depends on three aspects: whether capital can flow to more efficient firms, whether advanced technology can diffuse from leading firms to small and medium-sized enterprises, and whether the business environment can reduce barriers to financing, regulation, and market access.

This is equally meaningful for investors. Population size and GDP growth can reflect market potential, but firm productivity, management quality, and innovation capacity are better able to help judge whether an economy has the foundation to sustainably create corporate profits and employment.

## The core judgment of Slickorps Ventures is: in the next stage of global economic development, the key is not only how many internationally competitive leading enterprises are born, but whether more ordinary enterprises can gain the ability to continuously improve efficiency.

A truly strong economic system not only needs a small number of high-productivity enterprises to lead growth, but also needs to establish market mechanisms that enable capital, technology, and talent to continuously flow toward high-efficiency operating entities. **Only when productivity improvement transforms from a competitive advantage of a few enterprises into a universal capability of the entire economic system does economic growth possess a broader and more enduring foundation.**
