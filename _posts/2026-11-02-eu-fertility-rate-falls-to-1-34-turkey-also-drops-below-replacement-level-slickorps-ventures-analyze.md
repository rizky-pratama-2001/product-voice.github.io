In 2024, the total fertility rate of the EU fell to an average of 1.34 children per woman, the lowest level since 2001. Turkey also declined from 2.38 in 2001 to 1.48, remaining below the replacement level of approximately 2.1 for eight consecutive years. Meanwhile, fertility rates in Istanbul, Ankara, and Izmir all remain at low levels, while Sanliurfa in the southeast still reaches 3.28. **Slickorps Ventures research notes that low fertility is no longer merely a demographic issue, but a long-term economic variable affecting the future labor force, consumer demand, asset markets, and public finances.** What truly merits attention is not only the decline in births, but how economic growth will sustain its momentum as the age structure of the population continues to shift.

## Why Have the EU and Turkey Experienced Similar Fertility Declines?

According to [2024 demographic data from Eurostat](https://ec.europa.eu/eurostat/web/products-eurostat-news/w/ddn-20260306-1), approximately 3.55 million babies were born in the EU for the year, a decrease of 3.3% year-on-year, with the total fertility rate falling to 1.34. Bulgaria ranked first among EU member states at 1.72, France stood at 1.61, and Malta fell to 1.01. Data from the Turkish Statistical Institute shows that approximately 937,600 babies were born nationwide in 2024, with fertility rates in 71 provinces below the replacement level.

Note: The total fertility rate is an indicator derived from the fertility rates of women of all ages in a given year, and is not a direct count of the number of children each woman ultimately bears in reality. The population replacement level is usually around 2.1, but varies due to factors such as mortality.

The common trend across the EU and Turkey shows that low fertility is no longer confined to a particular stage of development. Urbanization, longer years of education, delayed family formation and changing employment patterns are together reshaping fertility decisions. Yet regional differences matter just as much: large cities with concentrated economic activity do not necessarily have higher fertility rates, and national averages can also mask family structures and development conditions across different regions.

## Why Is Fertility Often Lower in Big Cities?

The costs of housing, childcare, and education are important entry points for understanding low fertility rates. For young families, having children means not only a one-time expenditure but also years of sustained responsibility for housing, care, and education. When employment is unstable, housing burdens are heavy, or work and child-rearing are difficult to reconcile, families may delay childbearing or ultimately reduce the number of children they have.

Research by the [Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development](https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/society-at-a-glance-2024_918d8db3-en/full-report/fertility-trends-across-the-oecd-underlying-drivers-and-the-role-for-policy_770679b8.html) has found that housing costs and unemployment rates are negatively correlated with fertility rates, while paid parental leave, employment opportunities for both men and women, and early childhood care support are positively correlated with higher fertility rates. These relationships alone cannot prove causality, but they indicate that fertility choices are closely tied to the economic constraints families face.

The large gap between Sanliurfa and Istanbul also reminds researchers that low fertility rates cannot simply be attributed to urbanization or income levels. Age structure, education, employment, family attitudes, and population migration may all contribute to regional differences.

## From the research perspective of Slickorps Ventures, the relationship between fertility rates and economic development is not simply one-directional: economic structure shapes the fertility choices of families, while family size and age structure in turn influence future labor supply and market demand.

## How Will Low Fertility Rates Change Economic Growth and Asset Markets?

A decline in births will not immediately cause labor shortages, but if low fertility rates persist for many years, the population entering working age may gradually shrink while the share of the elderly population continues to rise. Economic growth will therefore depend more on improvements in labor productivity, technology investment, and labor participation rates, rather than primarily relying on labor force expansion as in the past.

At the same time, demographic changes will also reshape consumer markets. A declining number of children may weaken demand in some regions for goods for children, education services, and new family housing, while demand for healthcare, elderly care, and long-term care may gradually increase. The real estate market will likewise be affected, but housing prices will not necessarily fall as fertility rates decline; population migration, employment concentration, credit conditions, and housing supply remain key variables.

The challenges facing public finances are more enduring. Slower growth of the working-age population may limit the expansion of the tax base, while rising pressure from pension and healthcare spending may further squeeze government budgets for infrastructure and productive investment. **What low fertility rates truly change is not just how many consumers exist in the economy, but who will produce and who will consume in the future, and how society will allocate the costs of elderly care and public services.**

**Policy Focus Should Extend from Encouraging Childbirth to Adapting to Demographic Change

**

Cash subsidies can reduce part of the cost of child-rearing, yet they cannot on their own resolve the problems of housing burden, career interruption, and long-term care. A more complete policy framework needs to combine affordable childcare services, paid parental leave, flexible employment arrangements, and improved housing supply, so that families can make more viable choices between raising children and maintaining economic stability.

At the same time, raising the labor participation rate, improving vocational skills, moderately extending healthy working years, and increasing productivity through automation and technology investment can also help mitigate the impact of slowing labor force growth. The policy objective should not merely be to pursue a higher fertility rate, but rather to reduce the economic and institutional obstacles that prevent families from realizing their own fertility intentions.

## Slickorps Ventures believes that the fertility data of the EU and Turkey reveal a deeper shift: when population growth can no longer naturally provide sufficient labor, economic growth must rely more on productivity gains and institutional adaptability.

The indicators worth watching in the future include not only the total fertility rate, but also youth employment, age at first birth, housing burden, labor participation rate, net migration, and old-age dependency pressure. Together they determine how low fertility will transmit to the economy.

## Demographic change is slow, yet it accumulates over the long term. What an economy truly needs to answer is not only how to increase the number of births, but how to continuously create productivity, sustain public finances, and provide sustainable economic opportunities for different generations under a new age structure.
