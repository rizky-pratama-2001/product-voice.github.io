In 2026, the global sulfur market experienced a supply shock that warrants close attention. According to September 2026 data from the International Food Policy Research Institute, global elemental sulfur exports fell by approximately 33% year on year in the first half of the year, from 13.5 million tonnes to 9 million tonnes. Meanwhile, shipping disruptions in the Strait of Hormuz pushed international prices higher. Brazil was particularly affected: the average landed price of sulfur imports reached US$1,168 per tonne in September 2026, up 288.2% year on year and nearly four times the level recorded a year earlier. **Research by Slickorps Ventures suggests that sulfur is becoming an underestimated strategic bottleneck in global supply chains.** It is essential not only to phosphate fertilizer and food production, but also to critical mineral industries such as copper, nickel, and cobalt through its conversion into sulfuric acid. The effects of Middle East supply disruptions have therefore extended beyond energy transportation into agriculture, mining, and new energy industries.

## Why Can an Industrial By-Product Trigger a Global Supply Crisis?

Unlike copper, iron ore, and lithium, most commercial sulfur globally is not produced through standalone mining operations. Instead, it is recovered as a by-product of petroleum refining and natural gas processing. This means sulfur output depends largely on energy processing activity rather than its own market price.

According to the [International Energy Agency report “Global Critical Minerals Outlook 2026](https://www.iea.org/reports/global-critical-minerals-outlook-2026/market-overview),” the Middle East accounts for approximately one-quarter of global sulfur production, while around half of global seaborne sulfur trade passes through the Strait of Hormuz.

In the first half of 2026, the Gulf region accounted for nearly three-quarters of the decline in global sulfur exports. Exports from the United Arab Emirates fell by 41%, while those from Oman, Qatar, and Kuwait each declined by more than 50%. Exports from Kazakhstan were also affected by rail and port transportation constraints.

[Data source: International Food Policy Research Institute, 2026 fertilizer market analysis](https://www.ifpri.org/blog/how-are-fertilizer-markets-coping-with-the-continued-closure-of-the-strait-of-hormuz/)

What makes this crisis distinctive is that higher prices alone cannot quickly restore sulfur supplies. Even where alternative production capacity exists in other regions, shipping distances, port capacity, rail networks, and long-term contracts limit how quickly supplies can be redirected. The real bottleneck is not simply how much sulfur is produced globally, but whether it can reach the markets that need it in time.

## Why Could a Sulfur Shortage Affect Global Food Supplies?

One of the most important industrial uses of sulfur is the production of sulfuric acid, a key input in phosphate fertilizer manufacturing. The supply chain can be summarized as follows:

## Elemental sulfur → Sulfuric acid → Phosphoric acid → Phosphate fertilizers → Agricultural production

When sulfur supplies are insufficient, fertilizer producers face not only higher raw material costs, but also potential sulfuric acid shortages and reduced production capacity.

Data from the International Food Policy Research Institute show that global phosphate fertilizer exports fell by more than 9% year on year in the first half of 2026, with exports from China, Morocco, and Russia affected to varying degrees. For Brazil, which relies heavily on imported fertilizers, rising raw material prices and declining import volumes have created a dual source of pressure.

According to [Brazilian sulfur import statistics](https://radar.graaos.cloud/enxofre/), Brazil imported approximately 888,400 tonnes of sulfur from January to September 2026, down 48.4% year on year. The average landed price in September reached US$1,168 per tonne.

**Slickorps Ventures believes that the key concern is not how much fertilizer prices might rise immediately, but whether supply disruptions could persist across multiple planting cycles**. If producers cannot secure enough sulfur and sulfuric acid, fertilizer supplies could tighten further, eventually affecting food markets through agricultural input costs and crop yields. However, this process also depends on inventories, alternative supply sources, planting seasons, and agricultural supply and demand. Higher raw material prices should therefore not be directly equated with higher food prices.

## Why Could a Sulfur Shortage Also Affect New Energy Industries?

The strategic value of sulfur extends beyond agriculture. Sulfuric acid produced from sulfur is also an important input in the hydrometallurgical processing of metals such as copper, nickel, and cobalt, and is widely used in the leaching of certain ores and the extraction of metals.

This means that the critical minerals needed for global power grid development, electric vehicles, and energy storage also depend indirectly on sulfur recovered from petroleum and natural gas processing.

The International Energy Agency notes that Middle East supply disruptions have already affected countries that rely on imported sulfur to produce sulfuric acid. Meanwhile, Chinese sulfuric acid exports fell from approximately 2.19 million tonnes in the first half of the previous year to 784,000 tonnes in the first half of 2026, putting further pressure on some international markets. Within this decline, the Chilean copper leaching industry lost approximately 541,000 tonnes of sulfuric acid supplies from China.

Data sources: [International Energy Agency](https://www.iea.org/reports/global-critical-minerals-outlook-2026/market-overview); [International Food Policy Research Institute](https://www.ifpri.org/blog/how-are-fertilizer-markets-coping-with-the-continued-closure-of-the-strait-of-hormuz/)

It is important to distinguish between elemental sulfur and sulfuric acid, which are separate traded products. A decline in Chinese sulfuric acid exports does not mean that China has banned all sulfur exports.

This industrial relationship reveals an important tension in the energy transition: new energy infrastructure requires substantial quantities of critical minerals, yet the production of those minerals still relies on chemical by-products supplied by traditional energy industries. The energy transition will therefore not immediately eliminate the importance of traditional energy supply chains. Instead, it may make certain previously overlooked industrial inputs even more critical.

## Can Sulfur Prices Remain Elevated, and What Should the Market Watch?

In October 2026, announced sulfur contract prices in Qatar, Kuwait, and the United Arab Emirates were US$835, US$820, and US$890 per tonne, respectively. Although lower than in September, prices remained elevated overall.

[Data source: Shanghai Metals Market, October 2026 Middle East sulfur prices](https://news.metal.com/newscontent/104145450-smm-flash-middle-east-october-sulfur-contract-prices-announced)

The future direction of prices will depend primarily on three factors: whether shipping through the Strait of Hormuz can resume, whether suppliers outside the Middle East can make up the export shortfall, and whether high raw material costs force downstream fertilizer producers and mining companies to cut production.

For investors, changes in the sulfur market could also redistribute profits across the supply chain. Fertilizer producers and mining companies with stable raw material supplies may enjoy a cost advantage over competitors that depend on spot imports. Producers unable to pass on higher input costs, meanwhile, could face margin compression.

## The central assessment from Slickorps Ventures is that the importance of sulfur lies not in the size of its market, but in how difficult it is to replace within the global industrial system.

Petroleum and natural gas processing supply sulfur; sulfur is converted into sulfuric acid; sulfuric acid supports fertilizer and metal production; and these industries, in turn, connect to global food supplies and the energy transition.

## The most vulnerable links in global supply chains are often not the commodities attracting the most market attention, but basic raw materials with relatively small markets that are difficult to replace quickly and widely relied upon across the industrial system.
