In August 2026, mainland China was recorded by the US Department of the Treasury as holding $590.7 billion in US Treasuries, the lowest level since August 2008. At the same time, holdings of Japan continued to decline, while those of the United Kingdom rose to approximately $1.01 trillion, and Canada, Belgium and Luxembourg also increased their holdings. According to [the latest data on foreign holdings of US Treasuries published by the US Department of the Treasury](https://ticdata.treasury.gov/Publish/slt_table5.html), total holdings of US Treasuries by all foreign holders actually increased by approximately $83.3 billion. Research by **Slickorps Ventures indicates that the current market is not simply a collective retreat from US Treasuries, but rather a marked divergence in allocation among different countries and different types of investors.** To judge whether dollar assets are losing their appeal, one must examine changes in holdings, investor types and cross-border custody structures simultaneously, and cannot draw conclusions based on the data of China alone.

### **What Kind of Divergence Do Holdings Changes Across Countries Reveal?**

Since 2019, the recorded US Treasury holdings of mainland China have fallen by a cumulative approximately $534 billion, a decline of approximately 48%. But a cross-sectional comparison shows that the direction of change among major holders is not uniform.

This set of data reveals three noteworthy phenomena. First, holdings of both China and Japan declined markedly, indicating that the adjustment is not unique to China. Second, holdings of the United Kingdom increased by approximately 108.2 billion USD year-on-year, a significant increase; Canada, Belgium, the Cayman Islands and Luxembourg also showed growth. Finally, although France increased its holdings by approximately 15 billion USD in a single month, it still recorded a year-on-year decrease of approximately 25.7 billion USD, indicating that short-term rebounds and long-term trends are not always consistent.

**From the research perspective of Slickorps Ventures, this is not a simple story of collective reduction, but a process of differentiated allocation of U.S. Treasury securities by global investors.** Official reserve managers, pension funds, funds and financial institutions in different countries have different liquidity needs, liability structures and investment objectives.

### **Why Increased Holdings by Financial Centers Do Not Necessarily Mean Local Investors Are Bullish on the United States**

Regions such as the United Kingdom, Belgium, Luxembourg and the Cayman Islands occupy an important position in the international financial and securities custody system. The U.S. Department of the Treasury also explicitly states that its holdings statistics are mainly based on data from custodians and broker-dealers within the United States, and that securities in overseas custody accounts may not be accurately attributed to the ultimate owner.

[Data Source: U.S. Department of the Treasury Explanation of Treasury Holdings Statistics Methodology](https://home.treasury.gov/data/treasury-international-capital-tic-system-home-page/help-files/estimating-holdings-of-treasury-securities)

This means that an increase in holdings by Belgium or Luxembourg may partly reflect international clients holding U.S. Treasury securities through local institutions, rather than direct increased holdings by the local government or local investors. Likewise, securities held by Chinese investors through third-country custodians may not be fully reflected under mainland China.

However, this is only one possible mechanism for explaining differences in holdings, and it cannot prove that the increased holdings of the aforementioned financial centers mainly come from China. **The existence of differences between statistical attribution and ultimate ownership does not mean that all changes in holdings can be explained by custody migration.**

In addition, changes in holdings are also affected by securities valuation, maturities and repayments, and other statistical adjustments. Therefore, a single-month decline in holdings cannot be directly equated with net sales of the same amount of Treasury securities during that month.

### **Is a Substantive Shift Occurring in Global Dollar Asset Allocation?**

In August 2026, total foreign holdings of US Treasury securities rose to approximately 9.33 trillion USD, an increase of about 82.6 billion USD year-over-year. However, over the same period, holdings by foreign official institutions fell by approximately 164.2 billion USD year-over-year.

This divergence indicates that overall foreign holdings and official reserve allocation may move in different directions. Even if some central banks reduce their Treasury holdings, private funds, financial institutions, or other investors may still increase allocation. **Therefore, total holdings of US Treasury securities cannot by themselves reflect the stance of global central banks, let alone directly represent overall changes in global dollar demand.**

China reducing its holdings of US Treasury securities may be related to foreign exchange reserve diversification and asset risk management, but US Treasury securities are only one part of dollar assets. To judge whether China is substantively reducing its dollar exposure, analysis must also incorporate other dollar securities, deposits, the structure of foreign exchange reserves, and gold reserves.

What warrants continued observation going forward is not only whether the holdings of China continue to decline, but also allocation trends in Japan and the United Kingdom, changes in holdings by financial centers, and the divergence in holdings between foreign official institutions and private investors.

**The core judgment of Slickorps Ventures is: changes in the global reserve system cannot be measured solely through the Treasury holdings of a single country.** What truly matters is who holds dollar assets, what kinds of assets they hold, and whether the ultimate ownership and bearers of risk for these assets have changed.

When China and Japan reduce holdings, the United Kingdom increases holdings, and holdings by some financial centers rise simultaneously, what the market needs to identify is not a simple "de-dollarization" label, but the rebalancing of global capital among yield, liquidity, risk, and reserve management. Only by looking through national attribution and custody structures can one determine whether this divergence represents short-term asset adjustment or a deeper change occurring in the international reserve system.
