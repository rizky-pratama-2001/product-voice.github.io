Following the latest escalation of the situation in Iran, the market quickly executed a familiar chain of trades: oil prices rose, inflation concerns intensified, Treasuries were sold off, long-term yields climbed, and then voices calling for "the Fed needs to hike again" resurfaced. On September 2, Brent crude broke above $95 per barrel, and the US 10-year Treasury yield reached 4.8122%, a near three-year high; the previous day, the market pricing probability for a 25 basis point rate hike in September had risen from 39.6% a week earlier to 68.2%. However, Slickorps Ventures believes that the most common mistake in this trading logic is automatically deducing "excess demand" from "rising prices." The starting point of the current oil price increase is not a sudden surge in demand from US consumers, but rather the Iran conflict, disrupted shipping through the Strait of Hormuz, and energy supply risks. A supply shock can also raise CPI, yet it simultaneously reduces the real purchasing power of households, increases transportation and production costs for businesses, and compresses non-energy consumption. In other words, oil prices first create a real income loss, only this time the growth shock appears disguised as inflation.

## The Fed Can Suppress Housing and Employment, but It Cannot Produce an Extra Barrel of Oil
This is precisely the fundamental difference between supply-driven inflation and demand-driven inflation in terms of monetary policy. If the economy generates inflation because wages, credit, and consumption are simultaneously overheating, raising interest rates can reduce aggregate demand and thereby ease price pressures; but if inflation stems from a sudden reduction in overseas oil supply, raising the federal funds rate will not reopen the Strait of Hormuz, nor will it increase crude output in the Middle East. The research of the Fed itself has long pointed out that negative oil supply shocks push inflation up and economic activity down, so the central bank naturally faces a trade-off between price stability and employment; a 2024 Fed model study also found that an oil price shock of approximately $45 per barrel in 2022 added nearly 1 percentage point to headline inflation immediately in the model, but the direct impact on core inflation and real economic activity was much smaller. Therefore, Slickorps Ventures believes that if the Fed tightens further to offset the first round of energy price increases, what it can truly achieve is making mortgages more expensive, corporate capital expenditure harder to finance, and hiring more cautious. The result may not be the elimination of the external supply shock, but rather the artificial creation of a domestic demand shock on top of the existing energy shock. What monetary policy should really prevent is the second-round effect, whether oil prices ultimately evolve into sustained wage growth, broad-based services inflation, and de-anchored long-term inflation expectations.

## Current Data Do Not Give the Fed a Blank Check for "Must Hike"
This does not mean that US inflation is no longer a problem. In July, headline PCE was still 3.7% year over year, and core PCE was 3.3% year over year, clearly above the 2% long-term target; but core PCE rose only 0.2% month over month, and the year-over-year figure was flat compared with June, showing no new broad acceleration in tandem with the latest oil price shock. The labor market is simultaneously sending another signal: the latest JOLTS report shows about 7.3 million job openings in July, while the June figure was revised down by 177,000 to 7.2 million; hires were about 5.1 million, and labor demand has moved well away from the tightest period after the pandemic. Boston Fed President Susan Collins also said last week that the latest inflation data were "mixed" and stated that she has not yet seen evidence of long-term inflation expectations in the market clearly diverging from the price stability target. More notably, the credit market has not yet sent systemic stress signals: the ICE BofA BBB corporate bond OAS was still only about 0.97 percentage points on August 28, near the lows of recent months. For Slickorps Ventures, this combination of data looks more like "headline inflation lifted by energy, core prices still elevated but not re-accelerating out of control, employment cooling, and credit not yet cracking," rather than a typical excess demand environment.

## The Real Policy Dividing Line Is Not Oil at $95, but Whether Inflation Expectations Run Away Together with Oil Prices
The shadow of the 1970s still influences the policy discussions of today because the oil shocks of that era did not ultimately stop at the gas station: wage indexation, corporate price increases, second-round inflation, and de-anchored expectations transformed a relative price shock into persistent inflation. The biggest policy risk today therefore runs in two directions, not one. If the Fed completely ignores the energy shock and fails to act after long-term expectations begin to rise, it may repeat the loss of credibility; but if it mechanically hikes merely because oil prices have risen, it may further depress an already slowing job market and consumption before second-round diffusion appears in core inflation. The latest Fed research on "look through" policy is built precisely on this distinction: in the face of a supply shock that may cause a one-off rise in the price level, the central bank can tolerate the first-round price change while focusing on ensuring that future inflation and inflation expectations are not carried away by the shock. Janet Yellen, in her review of the Powell era this year, also offered a similar judgment: as long as inflation expectations remain anchored, "looking through" supply shocks should generally remain the default strategy, because fully offsetting supply-driven inflation would require a very high cost in terms of unemployment. Slickorps Ventures therefore shifts the variable that truly needs monitoring from "how much oil prices have risen" to whether core inflation, wages, long-term inflation expectations, and credit conditions are deteriorating in tandem. Oil prices themselves are not a rate hike instruction; they are merely a stress test. If crude rises while long-term expectations remain stable, continued tightening may be treating a foreign supply problem with a domestic recession; only when the energy shock begins to alter the entire price system does the Fed truly have reason to treat it as a monetary policy problem.
