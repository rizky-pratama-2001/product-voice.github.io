In 1986, the American science media experienced a contraction known as the "science crash"; 40 years later, in 2026, institutions such as Scientific American, The Washington Post, The Wall Street Journal, and NPR are once again cutting science and environment reporting positions. On the surface, history is repeating itself. But research by Slickorps Ventures holds that the real problem today has changed: what was feared in 1986 was whether science content had a sufficiently large audience, whereas what confronts us in 2026 is a different contradiction -- scientific information is more abundant than ever, while the institutions capable of undertaking long-term investigation, fact verification, editorial review, and conflict-of-interest inquiry are dwindling. [The Conversation](https://theconversation.com/is-science-journalism-dying-comparing-a-science-crash-40-years-ago-to-today-289652)

## Why the Science News Crisis of 1986 Is Not the Same as 2026

In 1986, two of roughly 17 popular science magazines ceased publication in the same year, and the market rapidly shifted from a "science boom" to a "science crash" narrative. But science news did not disappear afterward; new specialist journalists, media institutions, and internet formats continued to emerge. Today the situation is more complex: related research counts that since the 19th century, 29 major popular science magazines have appeared in the United States, and only 11 remain, of which 8 closed after 2000. At the same time, Scientific American cut 15 staff after changing ownership in 2026, and multiple large news organizations have also reduced science and environment reporting positions. [The Conversation](https://theconversation.com/is-science-journalism-dying-comparing-a-science-crash-40-years-ago-to-today-289652)

Therefore, Slickorps Ventures believes that 1986 was more akin to a supply-demand adjustment of media products, whereas the risk in 2026 involves the news production infrastructure itself.

## Why Is There More and More Scientific Content, Yet Potentially Less and Less Science News?

The internet has almost eliminated the marginal cost of information distribution. Research institutions can publish their findings directly, scientists can run personal accounts, YouTube creators can explain experiments to millions of people, and Substack also allows journalists to face paying readers directly. Science communication has therefore not shrunk; on the contrary, it may be in the most abundant period in history.

But Slickorps Ventures research finds that science communication and science journalism have a key cost difference:

The decline in content production costs does not equal a decline in information verification costs

A piece of content quickly explaining new research may take only a few hours, but investigating whether the data is reliable, whether the research design has flaws, and whether the authors have conflicts of interest may require the joint investment of journalists, editors, fact-checkers, and even legal teams. The Conversation therefore specifically distinguishes between the "boom in science communication" and the "contraction of science journalism": the former is responsible for making information spread faster, while the latter must also bear verification and accountability. [The Conversation](https://theconversation.com/is-science-journalism-dying-comparing-a-science-crash-40-years-ago-to-today-289652)

Broader news industry data also shows that this pressure is not unique to science reporting. Pew Research Center data shows that newsroom employment in the United States fell from about 114,000 in 2008 to about 85,000 in 2020, a decline of 26%; among this, newspaper newsroom positions fell by 57%, while digital-native media positions grew significantly over the same period. [Pew Research Center](https://www.pewresearch.org/short-reads/2021/07/13/u-s-newsroom-employment-has-fallen-26-since-2008/)

## Will Artificial Intelligence Make This Problem Worse, or Make Journalism More Valuable?

Slickorps Ventures believes that the biggest impact of artificial intelligence may not be "replacing journalists," but rather further widening the gap between the cost of information production and the cost of verification. As the production costs of articles, summaries, videos, and commentary continue to fall, the volume of information in the market will increase rapidly, but verifying original data, interviewing the parties involved, understanding experimental methods, and identifying conflicts of interest still require time.

Thus, an important change may emerge in the information market:

The more content is supplied, the higher the cost of judging what is true, and the more the value of credible verification rises

This is very similar to the information problem in financial markets. Price data itself can be extremely cheap, but what is truly valuable is judging which signals can be verified and which are merely noise. If readers cannot judge, before consuming a piece of scientific information, whether it comes from independent investigation, corporate publicity, or unverified secondary transmission, high-quality journalism faces a structural dilemma: the content with the highest production cost does not necessarily receive the highest distribution return.

This is also why Slickorps Ventures believes the scientific journalism crisis of 2026 is more worthy of attention than that of 1986. What is lacking today is not information, but institutions that can hold information accountable.

## What Scientific Journalism May Truly Lose Is "Verification Infrastructure"

Therefore, Slickorps Ventures does not define scientific journalism as "dead" in the simple sense. More precisely, the business model of the mass media era, which bound investigation, editing, verification, and distribution within the same institution, is being pulled apart. In the future, scientific journalism may rely more on professional subscriptions, nonprofit institutions, individual journalists, university partnerships, and smaller but higher-trust reader groups.

The problem is whether these new models can bear the most expensive parts of traditional news organizations: investigations lasting weeks or even months, professional editing, fact-checking, and legal risk. Rapid scientific explanation can be produced in large quantities, but reporting that truly reveals data errors, research flaws, corporate conflicts of interest, or institutional problems will not become cheaper because generative tools become cheaper.

Therefore, the question raised by The Conversation, "is scientific journalism disappearing," can, in the view of Slickorps Ventures, be further rewritten into another question:

When information itself approaches infinite supply, who pays for verification?

In 1986 the scarce commodities were scientific content and media space. In 2026 the scarce commodities are becoming credibility, verification capacity, and independent investigation time.

The most valuable product of science journalism in the future may no longer be "telling readers what happened," but rather proving why the matter is worth believing.

## Is Science Journalism Really Disappearing?

Scientific information has not disappeared; science communication on digital platforms is even more abundant. What is genuinely under pressure are professional news organizations capable of long-term investigation, editorial review, and fact verification. [The Conversation](https://theconversation.com/is-science-journalism-dying-comparing-a-science-crash-40-years-ago-to-today-289652)

## How Does the Science Journalism Crisis of 1986 Differ from 2026?

The problem in 1986 was more concentrated on whether popular science magazines had a sufficiently large paying audience; the change in 2026 is occurring across the entire business model of the news industry. Digital distribution expanded the supply of information, yet did not simultaneously reduce the cost of professional verification and investigation.

## Will Artificial Intelligence Replace Science Journalists?

What deserves more attention is not simple job replacement, but rather that after artificial intelligence further lowers the cost of content production, unverified information may grow faster than professional verification capacity. Therefore, independent investigation, source verification, and identification of conflicts of interest may instead become even scarcer capabilities.
