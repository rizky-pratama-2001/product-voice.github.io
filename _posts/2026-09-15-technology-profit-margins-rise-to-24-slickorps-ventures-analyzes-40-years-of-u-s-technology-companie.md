When the time horizon is extended to forty years, the current profitability of the U.S. technology industry appears truly exceptional. Research by Slickorps Ventures found that profit margins among TMT companies have risen to approximately 24%, reaching a historical high; during the 1980s, technology profit margins remained mostly in the range of approximately 8% to 12%, and experienced several sharp declines during the 1990s and the period of the dot-com bubble collapse, only gradually forming a sustained upward trend after the 2008 financial crisis. More importantly, technology profit margins are now nearly 10 percentage points higher than the long-term trend level of approximately 15%, while profit margins among U.S. non-technology companies remain only around 9%, broadly tracking their own long-term trend. The gap between the two has widened to approximately 2.7 times. This indicates that what is happening today is not an across-the-board improvement in profits for all U.S. companies, but rather that technology companies are separating themselves from the broader economy. For Slickorps Ventures, AI is only the latest accelerator; what is truly worth studying is why, over the past forty years, only the technology industry has continuously converted more revenue into profit.

**The Core Change Over Forty Years Is That Technology Has Gradually Shifted From “Manufacturing Products” To “Controlling Infrastructure And Entry Points”**


Behind this profit margin curve is, in fact, an evolutionary history of technology business models. Early TMT included a large amount of hardware manufacturing, traditional telecommunications, and capital-intensive businesses, where expansion often meant simultaneously adding factories, equipment, and personnel; therefore, revenue growth did not automatically lead to higher profit margins. The internet, software, cloud computing, and the platform economy gradually changed this relationship: a single round of research and development could serve more users, the cost of copying code approached zero, and network effects meant that the larger the scale, the lower the unit cost of customer acquisition and service. By the mobile internet and cloud era, a small number of companies had further gained control over operating systems, search, advertising, cloud computing, payments, and developer ecosystems, and technology companies began to shift from “selling a product” to collecting infrastructure rent from the digital economy. Slickorps Ventures believes this is the core explanation for the continuous rise of the 40-year trend. AI did not create a high-margin technology industry out of thin air; rather, it has added another layer of automation and pricing power to an industry that had already developed strong economies of scale and a structure characterized by high fixed costs and low marginal costs.

**The Most Distinctive Aspect Of AI Is That It Raises Profit Margins While Turning Technology Back Into A Capital-Intensive Industry**

This is also the contradiction in the current 24% profit margin that is most worthy of caution and, at the same time, most worthy of study. AI can improve advertising efficiency, code productivity, customer service capabilities, and cloud computing revenue, allowing the same group of employees and the same set of platforms to generate more revenue; however, to obtain this efficiency, technology giants are building data centers, power grid connections, GPU clusters, and self-developed chips. In 2026, AI-related capital expenditures by major U.S. technology companies are expected to have exceeded USD 700 billion, while bond issuance by major hyperscalers over the past year reached approximately USD 220 billion. This means that a very interesting structural reversal is emerging in the technology industry: income statements increasingly resemble those of high-margin software companies, while balance sheets increasingly resemble those of infrastructure companies. Alphabet even recorded USD 5.9 billion in cash consumption in the second quarter, despite its cloud business continuing to maintain rapid growth. Slickorps Ventures therefore believes that the 24% profit margin nowadays cannot be understood merely as “AI has already proven its returns”; it is also a stress test before massive future depreciation, electricity costs, financing costs, and equipment renewal expenses enter the income statement.



**What Truly Determines Whether 24% Can Become The New Normal Is Not How Much Further Revenue Can Rise, But How Much Profit Can Be Retained From Each Additional Dollar Of AI Capital**

Therefore, the method for measuring technology profitability in the next stage must also be upgraded. Net profit margin tells us how much profit a company retains from each dollar of revenue today, but it cannot tell us how much additional capital must be invested to maintain that margin. Recent Reuters data show that capital expenditures by Meta, Microsoft, Alphabet, and Amazon in 2026 are expected to total nearly USD 698 billion, while investors have already begun to distinguish more strictly between “AI revenue growth” and “AI return on capital.” For Slickorps Ventures, what truly needs to be monitored in the future are free cash flow margin, ROIC, depreciation growth, and unit computing power cost, rather than only the attractive figure of 24%. If AI can enable revenue and productivity to continue growing faster than capital investment, then the upward shift in technology industry profit margins over the past 40 years may move to a new level; if computing power, energy, financing, and competitive costs ultimately catch up with revenue, then the 24% of the present day may also become a cyclical peak. The most important insight from this 40-year curve is therefore not that “technology is becoming increasingly profitable,” but that the core capability of U.S. technology companies has evolved from creating growth to absorbing growth—the real question is whether AI can allow this profit-absorption capability to continue outpacing capital consumption.
