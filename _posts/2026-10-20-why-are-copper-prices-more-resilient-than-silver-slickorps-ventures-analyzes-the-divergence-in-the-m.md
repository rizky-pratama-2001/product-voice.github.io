In October 2026, a notable divergence emerged in the global metals market. Silver prices came under pressure amid shifts in rate expectations and investment sentiment, while copper prices demonstrated greater resilience. CME data shows that September silver futures fell approximately 9.6%, while copper futures declined only about 1.0% over the same period, a performance gap of 8.6 percentage points. Slickorps Ventures research finds that this divergence reveals a shift in the pricing logic of the metals market: precious metals are more susceptible to monetary policy and financial capital flows, while the price support for industrial metals is increasingly reflecting the importance of physical demand, inventory distribution, and supply chain security. Particularly noteworthy is that resilient copper prices do not mean the global copper supply is in full shortage. Even if a global surplus persists, as long as inventories are concentrated in specific regions, other markets may still face supply tightness. Prices are reflecting a more complex question: how much copper the world has, and how much copper the market can obtain in a timely manner, have become two distinct dimensions of judgment.

## Why Can Copper Prices Remain Resilient While Silver Slips?

A chart based on Bloomberg data shows that since 2025, copper and silver prices have repeatedly exhibited similar trajectories, but entering the second half of 2026, the two gradually diverged. Although the chart uses different left and right vertical axes, making it impossible to directly judge cumulative return differences from curve heights, the actual performance of the September futures market has already proven that copper prices have recently shown stronger downside resistance.

Slickorps Ventures trading team believes that understanding this divergence requires first distinguishing the different weights of financial attributes and industrial attributes in the pricing of the two metals.

Silver possesses both precious metal and industrial raw material attributes. On one hand, its demand comes from photovoltaics, electronic devices, and other industrial uses; on the other hand, investors also use silver for precious metal allocation, inflation risk management, and market volatility trading. When the dollar strengthens, bond yields rise, and especially when real rate expectations increase, precious metals that generate no interest income often face greater holding cost pressure. Adjustments in capital positioning may further amplify the price volatility of silver.

The demand structure of copper is more directly connected to the real economy. Power grid construction, industrial equipment, manufacturing upgrades, and data center expansion all require continuous consumption of copper materials. These demands are typically tied to long-term capital investment and infrastructure construction plans, and are difficult to fully eliminate through price changes in the short term. The September report from CME also noted that the demand performance and inventory declines in China provided some support for copper prices, keeping them relatively stable amid volatility.

However, resilient copper prices cannot directly prove that global industrial demand is in full recovery. Copper remains a typical cyclical commodity; a slowdown in manufacturing investment, weakening real estate activity, or declining industrial production could all affect actual consumption. Slickorps Ventures conducted further research on the limits of the downside space for copper prices

The answer may lie more within the supply chain itself, rather than in any single economic growth indicator.

## The Global Copper Market Is Still in Surplus, So Why Are Some Regions Facing Supply Tightness?

The forecast updated by the International Copper Study Group on October 14 shows that the global refined copper market is expected to see a supply surplus of about 104,000 tons in 2026, and the surplus could further widen to 368,000 tons in 2027. Judging from total annual global supply and demand, the copper market does not appear to show signs of a broad shortage.

But the research team at Slickorps Ventures points out that the annual supply-demand balance can only answer how much copper the world produced and how much it consumed, yet it cannot fully reflect where that copper is actually stored, or whether it can smoothly reach the markets that need it.

On October 14, Reuters reported that uncertainty over US import tariff policy has prompted traders to continuously ship copper to the United States. Macquarie estimates that copper inventories inside and outside US exchanges total about 1.6 million tons, while warehouses recognized by US commodity exchanges already hold about 70% of the copper inventory visible across global exchanges. A large amount of copper concentrated in the United States has resulted in ample local inventories, yet has left supply available to other regions relatively tight.

This phenomenon reveals an important pricing mechanism in the metals market: **a global supply surplus and regional supply tightness can exist simultaneously.**

When the US copper price carries a sufficient premium relative to the London market, traders can cover transportation, insurance and warehousing costs through the cross-market spread and earn arbitrage profits. Policy expectations thereby alter the international flows of copper, and even if total global production does not change noticeably, inventories may shift from one region to another.

However, this kind of price support is not without limits. Reuters points out that the arbitrage spread between the US and London markets has already narrowed markedly, and the economic appeal of continuing to ship copper to the United States is declining. If tariff policy becomes clearer in the future, or if the cross-regional spread narrows further, part of the price premium formed by the reallocation of inventories could weaken.

At the same time, copper mine supply is also constrained by factors such as declining ore grades, long development cycles, large capital investment, and the risk of production disruptions. The interplay between short-term shifts in trade flows and long-term mine supply constraints makes it increasingly difficult to explain the copper price on the basis of the global annual supply-demand gap alone.

The most noteworthy risk in the current copper market is precisely the mismatch that may persist between ample global inventories and tight regional supply.

## Silver Also Has a Supply Gap, So Why Has Its Price Fallen Instead?

The resilience of the copper price is noteworthy, while the performance of silver raises another question: if supply shortages can support metal prices, why has silver, which has long faced a supply gap, still undergone a significant correction?

In its market outlook released in February 2026, the Silver Institute projected that the global silver market would record a supply gap for the sixth consecutive year, with a full-year gap of approximately 67 million ounces. At the same time, industrial fabrication demand for silver is expected to decline by about 2%, one important reason being that the photovoltaic industry continues to reduce silver consumption per unit of product and is driving substitution of some materials.

Slickorps Ventures trading team further explained the changes in the silver market: a physical supply gap can provide price support, but it is not necessarily enough to offset the short-term pressure brought by changes in financial flows and demand structure.

When the market readjusts interest rate expectations, or when investors reduce exposure to precious metals, silver may fall rapidly due to changes in investment positioning. The profit-taking positions and leveraged trades accumulated during the previous rally may also amplify volatility during a price reversal. Even if annual supply remains below demand, inventory releases, increased recycled supply, and weakening investment demand may all ease short-term market pressure.

The deeper difference lies in the fact that the two metals do not see entirely identical changes in demand when facing technological progress.

Copper is widely used in power transmission and distribution networks, electrical equipment, and industrial infrastructure. Although some applications can be substituted with materials such as aluminum, technical performance, equipment design, maintenance costs, and compatibility with existing infrastructure mean that large-scale substitution often takes a considerable amount of time. By contrast, photovoltaic manufacturers can reduce silver consumption by improving production processes, reducing the amount of silver used per cell, and substituting some materials. Even if the scale of photovoltaic installations continues to expand, the demand for silver may not necessarily grow in tandem.

Of course, the use of silver in data centers, electronic devices, and the automotive industry still has room for growth, and its industrial demand has not lost long-term support. However, whether industrial expansion can translate into higher metal consumption also depends on changes in per-unit material consumption brought about by technological progress.

Therefore, the price divergence between copper and silver reflects two different sets of market pressures: silver is more sensitive to investment capital, interest rate expectations, and industrial silver-saving technologies, while the pricing of copper is simultaneously affected by infrastructure demand, regional inventory allocation, and the speed of supply adjustments.

## Supply gaps determine whether the market has a potential basis for tightness, but how inventories are released, how capital flows, and whether demand can continue to grow are what jointly determine the actual direction of prices in a given period.
