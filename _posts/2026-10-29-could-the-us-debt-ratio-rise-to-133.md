## Slickorps Ventures Analyzes Fiscal Risk and the Debt Cycle Under High Interest Rates

![](https://rizky-pratama-2001.github.io/product-voice.github.io/assets/img/2026-10-29-could-the-us-debt-ratio-rise-to-133-01-51b0a2a14c52.png)

In October 2026, the interest rate stress test published by the Congressional Budget Office revealed an increasingly prominent structural risk in the US fiscal system: if interest rates across all maturities remain 1.5 percentage points above the baseline forecast over the next decade, the cumulative federal deficit will increase by an additional approximately 6 trillion USD, and by 2036, federal debt held by the public as a share of gross domestic product could rise to 133.1%. Slickorps Ventures research notes that US fiscal risk is shifting from the expansion of debt scale toward the mutual reinforcement among financing costs, fiscal deficits, and capital market pricing. When the massive stock of debt makes interest expense an important source of fiscal pressure, the challenge facing the US is no longer merely whether it can continue to finance itself, but at what cost financial markets are willing to provide funding over the long term.

[Data Source: Congressional Budget Office October 2026 Interest Rate Scenario Report](https://www.cbo.gov/publication/62809)

## Why Would a 1.5 Percentage Point Rise in Interest Rates Add 6 Trillion USD to the Deficit?

According to the baseline forecast of the Congressional Budget Office, by 2036 federal debt held by the public will reach 120.2% of the economy. If interest rates across all maturities remain 0.5 percentage points above the baseline, the debt ratio will rise to 124.4%; if 1.5 percentage points above, it will further reach 133.1%.

Of the additional approximately 6 trillion USD in cumulative deficit, 4.9 trillion USD comes from higher financing costs on existing debt, and another 1.1 trillion USD comes from interest generated by additional borrowing.

These figures reveal an important characteristic of high-debt economies: when the debt principal is sufficiently large, interest rate changes themselves may become a driving force of fiscal expansion. Even without adding new public programs, the government must bear higher financing costs. Debt risk thus gradually extends from a fiscal revenue and expenditure issue to the interest rate pricing mechanism of the entire financial market.

## Is America Entering an Interest-Driven Debt Cycle?

The US government must continuously issue Treasury bonds to finance new deficits and repay maturing debt. As Treasury bonds issued during the low-rate era gradually mature and new debt is issued at higher yields, the overall interest burden rises incrementally. The Congressional Budget Office projects that even under the baseline scenario, annual net interest spending of the US will still increase from approximately 1 trillion USD in 2026 to 2.1 trillion USD in 2036.

[Data Source: Congressional Budget Office 2026-2036 Budget Outlook](https://www.cbo.gov/publication/61882)

Slickorps Ventures focuses on the feedback mechanism this process may form: interest spending pushes up deficits, widening deficits increase the supply of Treasury bonds, and growing financing demand may in turn prompt investors to demand higher long-term risk compensation. If this feedback persists, the government will have to devote more fiscal resources to servicing existing debt, compressing the space available to respond to economic shocks and support public investment in the future.

Fiscal risk is therefore cumulative. The cost of past debt is affecting the current budget, while current financing conditions will in turn determine the degree of fiscal flexibility in the future.

## Why Might Fed Rate Cuts Fail to Ease Fiscal Pressure?

On October 1, 2026, the yield on the 10-year Treasury of the US was approximately 5.2%, markedly higher than the average baseline projection of 4.3% for the next decade by the Congressional Budget Office. However, what the Federal Reserve can directly influence is mainly the short-term policy rate; long-term Treasury yields are also affected by inflation expectations, Treasury supply and demand, and term premiums.

This means that even if the Federal Reserve cuts rates, long-term financing costs may not decline in tandem. If the market believes that fiscal deficits are difficult to converge, investors may still demand higher long-term yields, thereby offsetting part of the effect of monetary easing.

The deep-seated constraint facing US fiscal policy lies in the fact that the government needs low-cost financing, while monetary policy must maintain price stability. When inflationary pressure and debt financing needs coexist, fiscal objectives and monetary policy objectives may come into tension. The status of US Treasuries as a global reserve asset provides an important financing advantage, but it cannot fully eliminate this constraint.

## The Real Risk of US Debt: Does It Depend on Interest Rates or Growth?

The key to judging debt sustainability lies in the relationship between the average financing cost, the nominal economic growth rate, and the primary fiscal balance. When the financing cost remains persistently higher than the economic growth rate, and the fiscal balance after deducting interest payments remains in deficit, the debt ratio is more likely to keep rising.

This also explains why, even using the benchmark interest rate, the US debt ratio may still reach 120.2% by 2036. High interest rates amplify fiscal pressure, but structural deficits are the foundation of long-term debt expansion. Therefore, lowering interest rates can ease the financing burden, but cannot replace economic growth and fiscal balance adjustments.

It should be emphasized that 133.1% belongs to an interest rate stress scenario and is not a definitive forecast. The actual debt path will still be affected by economic growth, inflation, and policy adjustments.

Slickorps Ventures believes that the impact of this trend is not limited to US fiscal policy. As an important benchmark for global asset pricing, changes in US Treasury yields may transmit to corporate financing, equity valuations, real estate, and international capital flows. When fiscal risk pushes up the risk premium on long-term Treasuries, its cost may be borne jointly by the broader financial system.

The deep-seated change in the US debt problem is that fiscal sustainability is forming an increasingly close link with global capital costs. What will truly determine the degree of risk in the future is not only how much debt the US can issue, but whether economic growth and fiscal capacity can sustainably support the financing returns that the market demands.
