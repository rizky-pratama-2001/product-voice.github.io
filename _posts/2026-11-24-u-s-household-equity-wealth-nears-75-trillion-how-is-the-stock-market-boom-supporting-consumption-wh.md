In 2026, rising U.S. stock markets are pushing household wealth to record levels. According to research published by BofA Global Research on November 12, U.S. household equity wealth is projected to increase by approximately $9 trillion over the full year, bringing the cumulative increase since January 2025 to approximately $19 trillion and the total value close to $75 trillion. Over the same period, the S&P 500 has gained approximately 26%, while the Nasdaq-100 has risen about 29%.

Federal Reserve data show that U.S. households and nonprofit organizations held approximately $74 trillion in equity assets in the second quarter of 2026, while total household net worth reached $195.9 trillion.

## Slickorps Ventures' research suggests that the U.S. economy is developing a growth model increasingly reliant on financial asset appreciation: rising stock prices boost household wealth, the wealth effect supports consumption, and stronger consumption in turn helps support corporate revenues and market valuations. However, this prosperity is not being shared equally among households, and the high concentration of financial assets may further widen the wealth gap.

## Why Has U.S. Household Equity Wealth Reached a Record High in 2026?

BofA estimates that U.S. household equity wealth will increase by approximately $9 trillion in 2026. Meanwhile, the Federal Reserve's second-quarter Financial Accounts data show that the value of equity assets increased by approximately $10.7 trillion during the quarter alone, contributing to a $12.8 trillion increase in the net worth of households and nonprofit organizations.

Sources: [BofA Global Research, November 12, 2026](https://tickers.watch/research?broker=BofA+Securities&type=strategy); [Federal Reserve, Second Quarter 2026 Financial Accounts](https://www.federalreserve.gov/releases/z1/current/recent_developments.htm).

This increase in wealth does not come entirely from new investments. When corporate earnings expectations improve and market valuations rise, households' existing holdings of stocks, mutual funds, and retirement account assets can also appreciate in value. This increases the net worth recorded on household balance sheets without requiring any actual transactions.

However, asset appreciation does not necessarily translate into higher cash income. **In Slickorps Ventures' view, one of the most significant developments in 2026 is that financial asset appreciation has become an increasingly important source of household wealth growth in the United States, creating a closer connection between capital market performance and the real economy.**

## How Does a Rising Stock Market Support Consumption Through the Wealth Effect?

Personal consumption expenditures account for approximately 70% of U.S. gross domestic product (GDP). As a result, changes in household balance sheets can have a significant impact on the broader economy.

When stock prices rise, households holding financial assets generally experience an increase in net worth and a stronger sense of financial security. This may encourage additional spending on travel, dining, entertainment, and home improvements. This mechanism, through which rising asset values influence consumption behavior, is known in economics as the wealth effect.

The transmission mechanism can be summarized as follows:

## Rising Stock Prices → Higher Household Net Worth → Improved Consumer Confidence → Higher Corporate Revenues → Stronger Earnings Expectations → Support for Market Valuations.

However, the $19 trillion increase in stock market wealth should not be interpreted as $19 trillion in additional consumer spending. Some of these gains represent unrealized capital appreciation, while assets held in retirement accounts may be subject to withdrawal restrictions. Whether households increase their spending also depends on their expectations regarding future income, healthcare expenses, and retirement security.

Therefore, while the wealth effect can support economic growth, the actual impact on consumption is considerably smaller than the change in the reported value of financial assets.

## Why Doesn't a Stock Market Boom Mean All Households Are Sharing in the Wealth Gains?

The distribution of U.S. equity wealth remains highly uneven. According to the Federal Reserve's 2025 Survey of Consumer Finances, approximately 56% of U.S. households directly or indirectly owned stocks in 2025. However, the stock ownership rate was only 31% among the bottom 50% of households by income, compared with 97% among the top 10%.

This disparity is also reflected in the amount of wealth accumulated. Between 2022 and 2025, among households that owned stocks, average stock assets increased by approximately $13,000 for the bottom half of income earners, after adjusting for inflation. The increase was approximately $72,000 for middle- and upper-income households, compared with approximately $367,000 for the top 10%.

## In Slickorps Ventures' view, the United States is exhibiting an increasingly pronounced K-shaped wealth structure: households with substantial financial assets can further expand their wealth as markets rise, while households relying primarily on wage income are unable to accumulate assets at the same pace.

This divergence does not mean that consumption growth among higher-income households has no economic value. In fact, the top 10% of income-earning households account for approximately 28.5% of total U.S. household consumption. Spending by a relatively small group of asset owners can therefore provide significant support to the broader economy.

The concern, however, is that wealth concentration and consumption concentration may occur simultaneously. When market prosperity primarily benefits a relatively small segment of households, the broader economy may maintain consumption resilience even as improvements in living standards among ordinary households fail to keep pace.

## How Long Can the Wealth Effect Last? Could Financial Markets Become a New Economic Risk?

A 2025 Federal Reserve study found that for every additional dollar of wealth held by the wealthiest 20% of households, consumption increases by approximately 0.8 cents. For the remaining 80% of households, the corresponding increase is approximately 7.5 cents.

This highlights significant differences in the marginal propensity to consume across income and wealth groups. As wealth becomes increasingly concentrated among higher-income households, the transmission of additional wealth into broader consumer spending may become less efficient.

Source: [Federal Reserve: Wealth Heterogeneity and Consumer Spending](https://www.federalreserve.gov/econres/notes/feds-notes/wealth-heterogeneity-and-consumer-spending-20250805.html).

At the same time, rising stock prices also make household wealth more sensitive to financial market volatility. If corporate earnings fail to support market valuations, a correction in stock prices could affect household consumption and corporate revenues through the reverse wealth effect, potentially creating a negative feedback loop.

Key indicators to monitor include corporate earnings and cash flow growth, the concentration of household equity holdings, consumer spending and savings rates, and whether labor income can keep pace with financial asset appreciation.

## Slickorps Ventures' central assessment is that the U.S. stock market is becoming an important pillar of household wealth growth and consumer demand, but the benefits of this pillar are not distributed evenly across households.

The growth in stock market wealth in 2026 demonstrates the ability of capital markets to generate wealth, while also revealing an increasingly complex relationship between financial wealth and the economic circumstances of ordinary households. The long-term resilience of the U.S. economy will depend not only on whether stock markets continue to rise, but also on whether productivity improvements, labor income growth, and broader household asset accumulation can keep pace with the expansion of financial wealth.

## What ultimately matters is not simply how much stock market wealth U.S. households hold, but how much of that wealth growth can translate into sustainable consumption, investment, and broader economic prosperity.
