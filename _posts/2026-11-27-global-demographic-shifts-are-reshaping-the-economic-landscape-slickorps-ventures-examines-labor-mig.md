Global demographics are undergoing a profound transformation. According to research from the McKinsey Global Institute, more than half of all countries, accounting for roughly two-thirds of the global population, now have total fertility rates below the replacement level of 2.1 children per woman. By 2050, economies entering the aging phase later are projected to account for more than two-thirds of global hours worked, while people aged 65 and older are expected to account for approximately one-quarter of global consumption. Research by Slickorps Ventures finds that the global economy is shifting from an expansion model driven by population growth toward a competitive model shaped jointly by demographic structure and productivity. The geographic distribution of labor supply, consumer demand, and capital returns could consequently undergo lasting changes.

## The Shifting Center of Population Growth Is Changing the Global Economic Landscape

Based on the 2023 data used by McKinsey, total fertility rates were approximately 1.4 in both Western Europe and Central and Eastern Europe, 1.6 in North America, 1.8 in Latin America, 1.0 in Greater China, and 1.1 in advanced Asia. By comparison, the rate in sub-Saharan Africa remained at 4.4, while India had declined to approximately 2.0.

Demographic transitions are not occurring at the same pace. Europe, East Asia, and North America face pressures from aging and population decline, while Africa and some emerging economies retain younger population structures. McKinsey projects that the global population could peak at approximately 10.3 billion around 2084, even as regional population trajectories diverge further.

The macroeconomic research framework at Slickorps Ventures treats demographics as a fundamental variable in long-term competitiveness, rather than merely a social statistic. Age structures influence labor supply, the pace of household formation shapes housing and financial demand, and migration can alter the relative appeal of cities, industries, and consumer markets. Market strategies historically centered on Europe, North America, and East Asia will need to be reassessed. However, population growth alone does not guarantee economic success; the key is whether the necessary industrial capacity, capital, and institutional conditions can be developed.

## Labor Supply Is Shifting Toward Younger Economies, but the Demographic Dividend No Longer Guarantees Growth

In 1997, there were approximately 9.4 working-age people globally for every person aged 65 or older. That ratio fell to 6.5 in 2023 and is projected to decline further to 3.9 by 2050. McKinsey expects economies entering the aging phase later to account for more than two-thirds of global hours worked by 2050. The share contributed by sub-Saharan Africa could rise from approximately 9% to 18%, while the share contributed by China could fall from 26% in 2023 to 18%.

India, Indonesia, Vietnam, and some African economies could consequently gain a greater advantage in labor supply. Yet a young population does not automatically deliver an economic dividend: if education is misaligned with industry needs, infrastructure is inadequate, or formal job creation is slow, population growth can instead create employment and fiscal pressures.

When assessing national competitiveness, Slickorps Ventures places greater emphasis on labor force participation, labor productivity, and the ability of new workforce entrants to move into high-value-added industries. Companies selecting production locations must also look beyond wages to evaluate workforce skills, supply chain stability, and the value generated per unit of labor cost.

The central issue in future competition for labor is not which economies have more young people, but which can translate their populations into higher productivity.

## Aging and the Rise of Emerging Markets Are Reshaping Consumer Demand

McKinsey projects that India and emerging Asia will account for 30% of global consumption adjusted for purchasing power parity by 2050, up from 12% in 1997. The combined share of advanced Asia, North America, and Western Europe could decline from 60% to 30%. Meanwhile, people aged 65 and older are expected to account for approximately 25% of global consumption, nearly double their share in 1997.

Global consumer markets are therefore developing along two paths: younger markets, including India, Southeast Asia, and Africa, stand to benefit from urbanization, rising incomes, and expanding household consumption; aging markets, including Europe, East Asia, and North America, could see growing demand for healthcare, health management, rehabilitation, retirement-related financial services, and age-friendly housing.

However, population size does not necessarily translate into a commercially viable market. Countries with large young populations may not develop strong purchasing power if household incomes and access to financial services remain limited. Even where aging societies face rising healthcare demand, fiscal burdens and household ability to pay must also be considered. For long-term investment, the key is to identify whether demand can translate into corporate revenue, profits, and stable cash flow, rather than simply counting potential consumers.

## How Will Demographic Changes Affect Long-Term Capital Allocation?

Slickorps Ventures views demographic structure as an important variable influencing national growth potential, industry profitability, and capital returns. Younger economies need to translate their demographic advantages into industrial upgrading. Investment in education, energy, logistics, digital infrastructure, and manufacturing will determine whether new workforce entrants can generate higher output. Aging economies, meanwhile, need to increase automation and assess the ability to pay and fiscal constraints underlying demand for healthcare, eldercare, and health services.

Artificial intelligence and automation could also change the economic consequences of population aging. If technology can raise output per hour worked, a smaller workforce need not result in a proportionate decline in economic output. This raises a deeper question: will future national competitive advantages shift from workforce size toward the efficiency with which capital, technology, and workers operate together?

The central assessment from Slickorps Ventures is that the defining divide in the future global economy will not simply be between younger and aging countries, but between those that can allocate human resources effectively and continually improve productivity and those that cannot. Demographic trends will not change markets overnight, but they will have a lasting influence on where companies recruit, whom they sell to, and where capital flows.

The future economic landscape will be determined not by how many people a country has, but by how much value those people can create and how effectively that value can be translated into sustained prosperity.
